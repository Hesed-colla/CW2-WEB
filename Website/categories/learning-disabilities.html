Learning Disabilities

People learn in different ways; accessible teaching creates more paths to understanding and showing what we know.

Overview

Learning disabilities are a broad term for differences that can affect how a person acquires, organizes, understands, or uses particular academic skills. Examples may involve reading, writing, or mathematics. The term and its use can differ across educational and health systems, so it is helpful to understand what a person means when they use it.

A learning disability does not indicate a person's intelligence, effort, curiosity, or potential. People have individual learning profiles and may benefit from explicit instruction, assistive technology, accessible materials, or additional time. Every person's interests and strengths extend well beyond school tasks.

Common Characteristics

  • Reading accuracy, fluency, or understanding may require different instruction or support.
  • Spelling, written expression, or organizing ideas in writing may be challenging.
  • Working with number concepts, calculations, or mathematical procedures may take additional strategies.
  • Learning a skill may take more practice or a different way of presenting information.
  • A person may know an answer but find one particular way of demonstrating it difficult.
  • Support needs and areas of confidence can differ across subjects and over time.

Strengths & Talents

  • Creative, visual, or hands-on problem-solving
  • Curiosity and deep knowledge in areas of interest
  • Strong oral communication or storytelling
  • Persistence and self-advocacy
  • Innovative ways of connecting ideas

Learning disabilities do not predict a person's talents; recognize each learner's own strengths.

Tips for Support & Inclusion

  1. Present information in clear steps and more than one format when possible.
  2. Offer appropriate tools such as audiobooks, speech-to-text, or accessible text.
  3. Allow different ways to demonstrate understanding, consistent with learning goals.
  4. Break longer assignments into manageable parts and make expectations explicit.
  5. Give feedback that is specific, encouraging, and focused on useful strategies.
  6. Ask the learner what is working and include them in planning supports.

Remember: Every mind is a wonder

With access and respect, learners can show their knowledge in ways that work for them.
