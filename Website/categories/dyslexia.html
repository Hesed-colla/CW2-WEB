Dyslexia

People with dyslexia can learn, create, and shine. The right tools and teaching can make reading more accessible.

Overview

Dyslexia is a learning difference that can make some parts of reading and spelling—such as recognizing written words, connecting letters with sounds, or reading fluently—more challenging. It is not a sign of low intelligence, laziness, or a lack of effort. People with dyslexia have varied interests, abilities, and support needs.

Dyslexia can look different from one person to another. It may be noticed when a child is learning to read, or later as school and reading demands change. Only a qualified professional can assess a learner; difficulty reading by itself does not tell us why someone is struggling.

Things a Learner Might Experience

  • Matching letters with their sounds or reading unfamiliar words may take extra practice.
  • Reading words accurately or smoothly may take more time and energy.
  • Spelling or remembering the order of letters in words may be challenging.
  • A learner may understand a story better when they hear it than when they read it themselves.
  • These experiences vary; not every person with dyslexia has the same challenges.

Strengths & Interests

  • Interests and talents as individual as each learner
  • Knowledge and ideas that are not measured by reading speed
  • Creative ways to solve problems and share understanding
  • Enjoyment of stories, learning, and discovery in many formats

There is no single set of strengths that comes with dyslexia. Get to know the whole person, not just their reading profile.

Ways to Make Learning More Welcoming

  1. Teach reading skills clearly and in small, well-supported steps, with practice suited to the learner.
  2. Offer audiobooks, text-to-speech, or a person reading aloud when the goal is understanding ideas.
  3. Give extra time when reading is not the skill being assessed, and avoid asking a child to read aloud without checking first.
  4. Use clear layouts and let learners choose helpful tools; no special font or color works for everyone.
  5. Notice effort and progress without comparing reading speed with classmates.
  6. Ask the learner what helps and work with family, teachers, and qualified specialists.

Remember: Reading speed does not measure a person's ideas

Offer different ways to explore stories, show learning, and build skills—with encouragement and without shame.
