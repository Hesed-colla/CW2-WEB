People use “special needs” in different ways, often to describe support or access needs related to disability. Some people prefer more specific language, such as “disabled person,” “person with a disability,” or the name of a condition. When possible, ask and follow an individual's preference; no single phrase suits everyone.

These words describe different things and their meanings can overlap. A disorder is generally a term used for a health or developmental condition; disability can describe an impairment and the barriers a person encounters in their environment. People may use either or both terms for themselves. The words do not tell us everything about a person.

Person-first language (“person with a disability”) and identity-first language (“disabled person”) are both used. Preferences vary by person, community, and context. Use the language someone asks you to use, avoid pitying or outdated terms, and focus on the person rather than making assumptions based on a diagnosis.

Listen to disabled people, respect privacy, and ask what access would be helpful rather than guessing. Support clear communication, accessible materials, flexible participation, and the right to make decisions. Address exclusion when you see it, while respecting how the person wants the situation handled.

Offer practical, specific help and accept the answer you receive. Include the child in ordinary social invitations, learn their communication and access preferences from the family, and see them as a whole person. Avoid unsolicited advice or framing a child's life as a tragedy.
